CLAUSE: 1 — Scope
This section defines the purpose of the document, providing guidelines for inspection, maintenance, and repair of drilling and well servicing structures (masts/derricks, substructures) to maintain serviceability, supplemental to manufacturer instructions and API 54. It lists covered items and notes exclusions (crown block sheaves under API 8B, offshore derricks not covered for Category IV verification). Verification: Ensure that inspection activities follow this document as supplemental guidance.

CLAUSE: 5.3.1 — General Bolted Structure Requirements
EXPLANATION: Each part of a bolted structure must carry its share of the load; omitted or improperly placed parts can cause failure. During erection, bolts should be left loose enough to allow proper fit of adjacent members without distortion and then locked in stress to ensure correct alignment and load distribution. After erection and periodically, bolts shall be checked for proper torque. Instructions for installing clamps, pins, and tie‑down bolts shall be developed by a qualified person using manufacturer guidelines. Clamp installation instructions must include pretension values and tolerances. Bolt tensioning shall be performed with calibrated tensioning methods. Bolt length shall be such that the end of the bolt is flush with or outside the nut face when installed. Responsibility for erection and bolt installation lies with those performing the work.

REJECTION: N/A

CLAUSE: 5.3.2 — Bolt Pretensioning
EXPLANATION: The turn‑of‑nut method is always acceptable for bolts not intended for reuse; reuse is permitted only if specified by the manufacturer or a Professional Engineer. Bolt tensioning must be achieved using calibrated tensioning methods. Tie‑down components expected to be tensioned multiple times shall be designed with a pretension not exceeding 50 % of the bolt material minimum ultimate strength times its nominal cross‑sectional area. Bolts specified for pretension greater than 50 % of the bolt material minimum ultimate strength shall be used only once.
ACCEPTANCE: Pretension ≤ 50 % of bolt material minimum ultimate strength × nominal cross‑sectional area
REJECTION: Pretension > 50 % of bolt material minimum ultimate strength × nominal cross‑sectional area

CLAUSE: 6.1 — General Inspection Objective and Routine Checks
EXPLANATION: Inspections aim to detect defects such as cracks or mechanical damage that may indicate impending failure. Significant manufacturing defects must be repaired or replaced and reported to the manufacturer. Routine checks include: weld inspection for cracks; erecting mechanism deformation check; hydraulic circuit inspection and air bleeding per manufacturer instructions with adequate fluid and complete air removal; hydraulic cylinders, pipework, and hoses leak and seal/wear inspection; wire rope inspection for kinks, broken wires, or other damage, ensuring guylines are not fouled and lines remain in sheave grooves; load transfer mechanisms, guides, and scoping ram stabilizers checked for free operation, cleanliness, lubrication, proper positioning, and full engagement; unit leveling and foundation/support placement per manufacturer recommendations; crown sheave wear and lubrication check; bearing lubrication and condition check in sheaves, sprockets, pins, etc.; folding ladder position and free operation check; scheduled bolted connection tightness inspections during drilling/servicing.

CLAUSE: 6.2 — Inspection Categories (I–IV)
EXPLANATION: Category I: visual observation of the mast/derrick and substructure by rig personnel during operations for indications of inadequate performance. Category II: includes Category I plus a more thorough inspection of load‑bearing areas and sheaves for cracks, damage, corrosion, loose or missing components, and premature wear, performed during rig‑up operations. Category III: thorough visual inspection of all load‑bearing components and members, documented on Annex A–D checklists; includes observation of rig‑up/rig‑down for well servicing, truck or trailer‑mounted masts; results maintained in the permanent rig file. Category IV: includes Category III requirements plus: (a) critical joints subject to nondestructive examination; (b) equipment disassembled and cleaned to the extent necessary for NDT of all defined critical areas; (c) all welds (100 %) shall be visually examined; (d) welds in critical areas inspected by magnetic particle (MPI/MT), liquid penetrant (PT), radiographic (RT), or ultrasonic (UT) testing per AWS D1.1, with eddy current as an accepted alternative and MPI/PT used to evaluate eddy‑current indications; galvanized welds may require different techniques; cracks are generally identified through visual inspection on galvanized mast/derrick; (e) ultrasonic thickness test or other recognized method for internal corrosion on tubular primary load‑carrying members, with internal cameras on cable as an alternative. Mobile offshore drilling unit masts/substructures are exempt from Category IV; Category IV inspections are required for rigs disassembled for transport between locations (e.g., fixed platforms, tension‑leg platforms). Inspection results from Category III and IV shall be documented and maintained in the permanent rig file.
ACCEPTANCE: 100 % of welds shall be visually examined
REJECTION: <100 % of welds visually examined (any weld not visually examined)

CLAUSE: 7 — Repair – General Recommendations
Structural repair of drilling or well‑servicing structures must be carefully planned before work begins; the manufacturer or a qualified person (per 4.2.1) shall be consulted for approval of materials and methods, using accepted engineering practices to supervise repairs. Specific recommendations include: repairing or replacing damaged members per Table 2; using manufacturer‑approved welding procedures; attaching fixtures and accessories with suitable clamps or bolted foundations; not drilling, burning, or welding any member without prior approval; keeping girts, braces, and other load‑bearing members in place while the structure is under load; allowing field repair of all damage but performing major damage repairs in a shop‑like environment per OEM or equivalent specifications; and ensuring replacement materials, pins, and bolts meet OEM specifications or equivalents. These requirements ensure repairs restore structural integrity and safety while preventing inadvertent weakening of the structure. Verification consists of reviewing repair plans, approval records, welding procedure qualifications, and post‑repair inspections.

CLAUSE: Table 2-1 — Legs
A bow in a leg member exceeding the allowable limit indicates excessive deformation that could compromise load capacity and stability; therefore legs must be inspected for bow and repaired or replaced if the bow exceeds the limit. Verification is performed by measuring the bow over a 10 ft (3 m) length.
ACCEPTANCE: Bow less than 1/4 in. (6.4 mm) in 10 ft (3 m)
REJECTION: 1/4 in. (6.4 mm) bow in 10 ft (3 m)
---
---
CLAUSE: Table 2-1 — Braces
Excessive bow in brace members reduces their ability to resist lateral loads and may lead to buckling; braces are checked for bow over a 10 ft (3 m) span and must be repaired or replaced when the bow exceeds the allowable limit.
ACCEPTANCE: Bow less than 1/2 in. (12.7 mm) in 10 ft (3 m)
REJECTION: 1/2 in. (12.7 mm) bow in 10 ft (3 m)
---

CLAUSE: Table 2-1 — Overall alignment of structure
The overall alignment of the structure must remain square within tolerance; excessive out‑of‑square indicates distortion that can affect load paths and erection. Verification is made by measuring deviation from square over the relevant dimension.
ACCEPTANCE: Out‑of‑square less than 3/4 in. (19.1 mm)
REJECTION: 3/4 in. (19.1 mm) out of square
---

CLAUSE: Table 2-1 — Pin holes (LD.)
Pin hole diameters that are undersized beyond the permitted tolerance can interfere with pin insertion, cause stress concentrations, and hinder proper assembly. Holes are measured and compared to the specified diameter.
ACCEPTANCE: Pin hole diameter not under the specified diameter by more than 1/16 in. (1.6 mm)
REJECTION: 1/16 in. (1.6 mm) under specified diameter

CLAUSE: Table 2-1 — Corrosion
Excessive material loss or build‑up on pins changes their effective diameter, affecting fit and load transfer. For pins smaller than 3 in. (76.2 mm), the pin diameter must not exceed the specified size by more than the stated tolerance.
ACCEPTANCE: Pin diameter not over the specified diameter by more than 3/16 in. (4.8 mm) for pins <3 in. (76.2 mm)
REJECTION: 3/16 in. (4.8 mm) over specified pin diameter (for pins less than 3 in. [76.2 mm] diameter)

---
CLAUSE: Table 2-1 — Structural members and wire rope
For pins 3 in. (76.2 mm) diameter or larger, excessive wear or corrosion that increases the pin diameter beyond the allowed tolerance can impair fit and increase stress. The pin diameter is checked against the specification.
ACCEPTANCE: Pin diameter not over the specified diameter by more than 1/4 in. (6.4 mm) for pins ≥3 in. (76.2 mm)
REJECTION: 1/4 in. (6.4 mm) over specified pin diameter (for pins 3 in. [76.2 mm] diameter or greater)

CLAUSE: Table 2-1 — Exposure to heat
After exposure to elevated temperatures, sheaves, rollers, and bearings must turn freely, be free of cracks, and have groove dimensions within the manufacturer’s gage and depth limits. Excessive groove depth or out‑of‑gage grooves indicate heat‑induced distortion.
ACCEPTANCE: Sheaves turn freely, no cracks detected, groove within gage, and groove depth not greater than allowed by sheave manufacturer
REJECTION: Do not turn freely, have cracks detected, sheave groove out of gage, or groove depth greater than allowed by sheave manufacturer

REJECTION: In excess of 500 OF (260 oc)

CLAUSE: 7.2 — Corrosion
Corrosion damage that reduces the cross‑sectional area of a structural member by more than 10 % (or the percentage tolerance specified by the manufacturer) should be considered for repair. After removing corrosion to sound metal by abrasive blasting or mechanical cleaning, the affected area may be repaired by welding fill‑and‑grind, fish‑plating, or member replacement, followed by recoating. This limit ensures that remaining metal retains sufficient strength to carry design loads. Verification involves measuring the remaining cross‑sectional area before and after cleaning.
ACCEPTANCE: Cross‑sectional area reduction ≤10 % (or within manufacturer‑specified tolerance)
REJECTION: >10 % reduction in cross‑sectional area
---
---
CLAUSE: 7.3 — Fire and Heat Exposure
Following exposure to temperatures exceeding 500 °F (260 °C), the affected areas of the structure must be inspected for distortion. If the temperature exceeds the critical temperature of the steel grade, a qualified person must perform further examination of the affected area. This requirement prevents loss of strength or dimensional changes due to overheating. Verification includes temperature measurement (if available) and visual/dimensional inspection for warping, cracks, or groove changes.
ACCEPTANCE: Temperature ≤500 °F (260 °C)
REJECTION: Temperature >500 °F (260 °C)

CLAUSE: 10.1 — Procedure
To determine a load rating for a well‑servicing or drilling structure of unknown manufacture, the following steps shall be performed: (a) initial inspection to survey member sizes, geometry, and overall condition; (b) structural analysis in accordance with API 4F to establish a load rating; (c) repairs and refurbishment to achieve full structural integrity; (d) final inspection; (e) attachment of an identification nameplate; (f) documentation of the entire process. This procedure ensures that equipment of obscure origin can be safely placed into service with a verified rating. Verification consists of completing each step and retaining the associated records.

CLAUSE: 12 — Raising/Bridle Line Inspection and Replacement
Raising line life is limited by wear (number of raises), corrosion (time/environment), and damage (independent of wear/corrosion). Lines must be inspected before raising/lowering; rusty lines inspected/replaced as needed; end areas examined closely for corrosion; kinked, crushed, or otherwise distorted ropes replaced; replacement based on inspection and lift count; lines kept well lubricated with a compatible lubricant to reduce internal friction and prevent corrosion; protected from abrasion using drill‑pipe rubbers or tying‑back; in multi‑part wire‑rope substructure raising systems, remove from service if six or more broken wires occur in one lay, or three or more broken wires occur in one strand in one lay; any broken wire in a mast raising line requires replacement.

CLAUSE: 13.1 — General
Any mast that relies on guywires for stability must have all applicable guywires installed before work begins. Number, placement, and size should follow the manufacturer’s recommendations; if not shown on the nameplate, follow Figure 2; if obstructions prevent those patterns, a qualified person may develop an alternative pattern based on site‑specific hook load, wind load, and foundation adequacy, and the pattern must be available at the well site. All guywires must be 6×19 or 6×37 class, regular lay, IPS (minimum), IWRC wire rope per API 9B, and must not have been previously used in another application.

CLAUSE: 13.2 — Standing Ropes
Standing ropes (guylines, escape lines, pendant lines) must be removed from service when any of the following conditions exist: three broken wires within one lay length; two broken wires at the end connection in the strand valley; marked corrosion; corroded wires at end connections; end connections that are corroded, cracked, bent, worn, or improperly applied; or evidence of kinking, crushing, cutting, cold working, or bird caging. These criteria ensure compromised rope is taken out of service before failure.

Guylines in use must receive a visual inspection at least once each day. Additionally, a thorough inspection must be performed once each month, with a record made of any defects observed. Refer to API 9B for further guidance on wire‑rope care and inspection.

CLAUSE: 13.4 — Guy wire End Terminations and Hardware
End terminations must follow good practice and the current API 9B; never turn the rope back over a small‑radius eye—use thimbles or suitably sized sheaves. When wire‑rope clips are used, double‑saddle type clips are recommended and must be installed per the clip manufacturer’s torque instructions; if a sheave replaces a thimble, add one extra clip. Brackets that attach guywires to the mast or tubing board (when non‑standard patterns are used) must be verified to have sufficient capacity for the maximum anticipated loads. All hardware that remains in the live guywire system (shackles, turnbuckles, walking boomers, chain come‑a‑longs, load binders, etc.) must have a safe working load capacity of at least 40 % of the guywire’s breaking strength, and handles on devices such as walking boomers must be positively secured to prevent accidental release. Grab hooks or open hooks on guywire terminations are not recommended.

CLAUSE: 14.2 — Capacity Verification
The capacity of each permanent anchor must be verified within 24 months before starting work and rechecked if any changes occur that could reduce anchor capacity. Verification may be performed by pull testing or other engineering‑approved methods that give equivalent results. Anchors must be retested if deformation or damage is observed.
ACCEPTANCE: Verification performed ≤ 24 months prior to work commencement; rechecked after any capacity‑reducing changes.
REJECTION: Verification older than 24 months; not rechecked after changes that could decrease anchor capacity.
---
CLAUSE: 14.3 — Alternative Anchoring Practices
When substructures and base beams are designed and approved by the manufacturer as anchorage for mast guywires, the anchor capacity is determined by the dead weight of equipment and fabricated components (e.g., padeyes) through engineering calculations; pull testing of the base beam is not required. For screw‑in type anchors, capacity may be correlated to installation torque per the manufacturer’s instructions, and verification must be done using a mechanical method (pull‑tested or shear‑torque method). Anchors must be retested if deformation or damage occurs.
ACCEPTANCE: Anchor capacity derived from approved engineering calculations or torque method per manufacturer; verification method as specified.
REJECTION: Use of unapproved alternative anchorage without proper design approval; failure to retest after deformation or damage.
---
CLAUSE: 14.4 — Pull Testing and Site Preparation
When pull testing is used to verify anchor capacity, the pull direction must be in the plane of the anchor and wellhead at an angle approximating the guywire angle. The test pull must be maintained for at least two minutes after anchor movement ceases, using devices equipped with chart recorders to provide a permanent record. Measuring or recording devices must be calibrated annually or when damaged/repaired by a qualified independent service, with current calibration records kept with the equipment. Anchors must be installed so liquids drain away from the shaft; soil should be mounded and tightly packed around the shaft to keep fluids off subsurface components.
ACCEPTANCE: Pull direction approximates guywire angle; pull duration ≥ 2 minutes; device calibrated within the last year or after damage/repair; calibration records available; drainage and mounding as specified.
REJECTION: Pull direction not aligned; pull duration < 2 minutes; device not calibrated annually or after damage/repair; missing calibration records; improper drainage or insufficient soil mounding.
---
CLAUSE: 14.5 — Responsibilities of Contractor, Operator, and Testing Company
The rig contractor must ensure anchor capacities are verified before attaching guywires, that verification is less than 24 months old, and that anchor spacing and capacity suit the mast guying pattern and anticipated loading. The contractor must maintain guywires and end terminations in good condition, inspect anchors for damage/deterioration before rigging up, inspect surface ground conditions that might indicate reduced anchor capacity, align the rig relative to wellhead and anchors, place visible markers on each guywire, and replace soil around anchors in accordance with 14.4.
ACCEPTANCE: All contractor responsibilities fulfilled as described.
REJECTION: Any contractor responsibility not met (e.g., unverified anchors, outdated verification, poor guywire condition, uninspected anchors, ignored ground conditions, misaligned rig, missing guywire markers, improper soil replacement).
---
CLAUSE: 14.6 — Responsibilities of Well Site Owner/Operator
The well site owner/operator must install anchors per 14.1, provide anchor capacity verification per 14.2, replace anchors that are damaged, excessively deteriorated, or fail verification, maintain a permanent file record for each installed/tested anchor including installation dates, each capacity verification, pull test charts, and the verifier’s name and telephone number, keep the most current verification records on a weather‑resistant tag attached to each tested anchor indicating capacity and verification date, and place a visible marker on each anchor they furnish, install, or specify.
ACCEPTANCE: All owner/operator responsibilities fulfilled.
REJECTION: Any owner/operator responsibility not met (e.g., anchors not installed per 14.1, missing verification, not replacing failed/damaged anchors, incomplete file records, missing or outdated tag, missing anchor markers).
---
CLAUSE: 14.7 — Responsibilities of Party Conducting Capacity Verification
The party conducting anchor capacity verification must maintain test equipment in good working condition, provide required records to the well site operator/owner or contractor as applicable, install a weatherproof tag after each pull test indicating anchor capacity and verification date, and maintain and calibrate pull testing equipment at least once per year or following any damage/repair.
ACCEPTANCE: Equipment maintained, records provided, tag installed after each test, calibration within the last year or after damage/repair.
REJECTION: Equipment not maintained, records not provided, tag missing after test, calibration overdue (> 1 year) or not performed after damage/repair.

CLAUSE: 15 — Foundations for Masts and Substructures
Adequate foundations are essential for mast, substructure, and rig carrier stability. Foundation design must consider local ground bearing capacity, concentrated loads at mast/substructure/rig carrier points, supplemental footing needed to distribute those loads, and site preparation. Sites must be graded and drained to keep fluids away from the work area; drainage ditches under load‑bearing members should be avoided as they reduce effective bearing area. Wet conditions significantly reduce soil bearing capacity. Safe bearing capacity of local ground may be determined from Table 6 or via soil core tests, penetrometer, suitable soil tests, analysis, or empirical data. When using surface conditions to estimate bearing capacity, the soil must be homogeneous to at least twice the width of the supplemental footing; underlying soft layers to that depth govern bearing capacity rather than firmer surface soil.
ACCEPTANCE: Foundation design addresses bearing capacity, loads, supplemental footing, drainage, and soil homogeneity as required.
REJECTION: Foundation design omits any of these considerations; inadequate drainage; ditches under load‑bearing members; using surface bearing capacity without verifying soil homogeneity to the required depth.

CLAUSE: 16.1 — Low‑temperature Operation Limit
Mast raising and lowering operations have been demonstrated successfully at temperatures as low as –50 °F (–45.6 °C). To prevent brittle failure or increased susceptibility to impact, operations at or below this temperature must be conducted under a program of closely controlled inspection and handling procedures.
ACCEPTANCE: Operations conducted at temperatures ≥ –50 °F (–45.6 °C) with controlled inspection/handling, or at lower temperatures only when justified by engineering analysis and special procedures.
REJECTION: Operations performed below –50 °F (–45.6 °C) without the required controlled inspection and handling program.
---
CLAUSE: 16.2 — Welding Temperature Restriction
Welding of oilfield structural steel should not be performed when the ambient temperature is below 0 °F (–17.8 °C) because of the risk of cold‑cracking and insufficient fusion without proper preheat.
ACCEPTANCE: Ambient temperature during welding ≥ 0 °F (–17.8 °C)
REJECTION: Ambient temperature during welding < 0 °F (–17.8 °C)
---
CLAUSE: 16.3 — Preheat Requirement for Steel Parts
All commonly used steel grades require preheat when the parts being joined are less than 32 °F (0 °C). Some grades also need controlled interpass temperature and post‑weld heat treatment even above 32 °F; the manufacturer’s guidance must be followed.
ACCEPTANCE: Preheat applied when part temperature < 32 °F (0 °C) (and per manufacturer for other cases)
REJECTION: No preheat when part temperature < 32 °F (0 °C) unless manufacturer explicitly states it is unnecessary.
---
CLAUSE: 16.4 — Recommended Practices for Low‑temperature Operations
To minimize damage and impact loading during mast raising and lowering in cold weather, the following practices are recommended: (a) schedule operations at the warmest part of the day, using sunlight and wind considerations; (b) apply practical heating means (e.g., high‑pressure steam or hot‑air blowers) to mast attachment points; (c) repeatedly take up and loosen mast‑raising lines to ensure free movement; (d) warm up engines and verify machinery function to avoid sudden braking or jarring; (e) perform inspection and repair as per Sections 6 and 7, which are especially critical in cold conditions; (f) observe the welding temperature and preheat restrictions noted above.
ACCEPTANCE: All applicable practices (a) through (f) are implemented as part of the low‑temperature operation procedure.
REJECTION: Any of the practices (a)–(f) is omitted or not followed when low‑temperature conditions are present.
